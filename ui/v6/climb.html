Ranked, played properly

The Climb

How to play ranked Predecessor effectively when you already know the game — distilled from 200+ community guides, esports coaching practice, and the film of real ranked matches. This is not a beginner guide. It assumes you can lane, last-hit, and land your kit; it is about the decisions between the mechanics, because that is where games above Gold are won.

"Mastery is not achieved by winning more games than everyone else. It is achieved by making better decisions than you made yesterday."— The Wraith Codex, one of the community's best-regarded guides. The rest of this page is what those decisions are.

1 · Play the clock, not the kills

High-level Predecessor is scheduled. Every deep macro guide structures the whole game around objective respawns, and our own match films confirm the cadence — the schedule below is measured across ranked games, not folklore.

ObjectiveClockWhat it means for your plan
River buffsfrom ~3:00, ~every minuteContinuous side income. Whoever holds river tempo holds the map's cash flow.
Seedlingsfrom ~4:00, ~3-min cycleThe between-windows farm. An idle window with no seedling taken is income handed over.
Fangtooth5:00, ~5–6 min cyclePermanent, stacking, team-wide damage. It is a race counted in stacks, not a one-off prize.
Mini Prime (Mini Orb)~7:00 / ~13:00 / ~19:00A conversion buff — it exists to melt plated towers in the minutes after the take.
Orb Prime & Primal Fangtoothfrom ~20:00The game-enders. Late deaths near these windows hand the game over — death timers run ~40s+ by then.

The rules the clock creates

  • The third Fangtooth (~15:00) is a named power spike. Community consensus treats it as "your number one priority, no exceptions" — a permanent team-wide damage lead from the 15-minute mark. Plan the two minutes before it like a set play: wave state, wards, bodies.
  • Mini Prime is for converting, not holding. Take it and push immediately — the community calls it the "Giga Push." Dying with the buff is acceptable if it took towers or forced a cross-map reaction. Grade your take by what fell in the next window.
  • Deward before every objective attempt. The sentry bought on your first recall is an objective tool, not a luxury. Vision work is scheduled around the clock, not ad hoc.
  • Objective trading is real play. Enemy committed to Fangtooth? Take Mini Prime or their camps. Giving up the first Fangtooth to build a farm lead is a legitimate, stated strategy in high-level guides — as long as you actually collect the trade.

2 · Tempo economics — fights are loans, not wins

The scoreboard lies to climbers. A won fight is a loan of tempo that expires with the enemy death timers: if nothing is collected before they respawn — an objective, a tower, a wave crashed for a reset — the fight paid nothing. Film review across ranked games shows the same lost-game shape over and over: fights won, none cashed.

  • Cash every won fight. The question after a won fight is never "who do we chase" — it's "what falls before they respawn." Chasing kills after a won trade is the classic mid-ladder death.
  • Deaths are priced by what they cost, not counted. A 2-death game where both deaths directly preceded an enemy Fangtooth and an inhibitor was worse than a 7-death game of even trades. Watch what fell within the minute after each of your deaths.
  • Caught-out deaths are the cheapest thing to fix. Deaths outside any fight — walking a river without vision, farming a pushed side lane at 22 minutes — are pure tempo handed to the enemy for free, and they are a habit, not bad luck.
  • Fight on your item spike, not theirs. A fight taken one completed item down is a timing error dressed up as a skill matchup. If their carry just finished a core item and yours is 800g short, the correct fight is the one two minutes from now.
  • A lost fight that bought a cross-map prize is a trade, not a throw. Grade the whole map: 3 deaths bot while your splitter took two towers top can be a winning exchange.

A won fight spent on no objective is tempo left on the table.

3 · Your role's real yardstick

Most ladder players grade every role on the carry's scoreboard. High-level guides — written by the people who main these roles — grade very differently. Hold yourself to the yardstick your role's own best players use.

Jungle — tempo and the schedule, never gank count

  • Ganks are a byproduct of map control, judged by what they converted (a tower, an objective, a fed lane) — "forced early ganks waste time."
  • Clear efficiency is a studied skill: chain camps with zero downtime, drag camps to keep passives rolling, space ability procs across the clear. High-level junglers benchmark first clears to the second.
  • Invade windows are read off the clock: enemy jungler committed to Fangtooth, dead, or showing on the map means their camps are free.
  • The accountability line, from the community itself: "You are the jungler. When Fangtooth gets into enemy hands, it's your fault."
  • Playing an assassin? Farm their camps before even thinking about ganks, and in teamfights "let your teammates engage while you look for opportunities to single someone out." Patience is the assassin's mechanic.

Offlane — coach the lane state, not the lane

  • The lane splits into two games. Winning: poke, shove, and "look to roam when the wave is pushed — your presence is extremely valuable in fights across the map." Losing: farm safe, ward against the dive, follow roams or split — safe farm under tower beats a coin-flip trade. Know which game you're in before every decision.
  • Your known failure mode is external: "get camped by their jungler and watch your duo lane still lose." The answer is defensive itemization, aggressive warding, and patience — an offlane death pre-10 opens the river and costs more than any missed wave.
  • The kill pattern is a sequence, not a moment: poke, force the escape ("burn their Blink"), then re-engage on the dead cooldown. Escapes-forced is a real intermediate win.
  • Trades run through resources, not courage: let your damage-over-time tick in short trades, punish when they overextend, and count both kits' cooldowns before committing.

Midlane — the rotation hub

  • Mid is graded on shove-and-rotate, not on out-farming. The roam trigger is a crashed wave, not a clock — rotating off an un-pushed wave costs the lane twice.
  • Budget your mobility cooldowns for the farm loop, not just fights — the small tricks (holding an escape to jump back into lane after river buffs) are what keep mid's income and presence simultaneous.

Carry — farm-to-damage, and the one death that matters

  • The carry's own guides grade themselves on one number: caught-out deaths. "The worst case scenario is getting caught in a CC." Positioning is the mechanic; everything else is arithmetic.
  • Behind? The play is stated flat in high-level carry guides: "focus on safe farming, picks, and avoiding full-on engagements until you scale." Ahead? Maximize pressure — but in teamfights, "let your team initiate while you stay alive. One clean kill can win the fight — survival and execution matter."

Support — the three stances and the 3-item reality

  • Support economy is blunt: "most games you'll only get 3 to 4 items." Your first three slots ARE your build — and even skill-justified greed caps around three offensive items before you tank up.
  • Pick a stance and play it on purpose. The community runs three: peel (classic shield/CC bodyguard), damage-scaling ("magical power has way more multipliers than heal & shield power — do the math"), and the entry facilitator ("blink into the middle of the enemy team — be the flashbang for your entry team"). Each is legitimate; drifting between them mid-game is not.
  • Uptime beats moments: "an effect you can use only once every minute is no effect at all — frequency is king." The same logic prices your ultimate: save it for objective windows, and plan the exit before you press it.
  • Peel includes counter-CC: canceling the hook or the channel with your own stun is the highest form of peel. And shield preemptively — before expected damage, not after.
  • A roam is only as good as the cooldowns it joins — "rotating to midlane only benefits allies with available abilities." Check their ults before you walk.

4 · Wave control — how to freeze effectively

Freezing is the strongest lane tool most of the ladder never uses on purpose: you hold the enemy wave just outside your tower's range and refuse to let it move. They must walk past the river to farm — gankable, overextended, and losing gold and XP if they don't. You farm with an escape route behind you. A freeze is deny and gank setup in one.

Know your minions — the wave's damage lives in the back

Every wave is 3 melee + 3 ranged, and they are not interchangeable (numbers per the community wiki): ranged minions hit harder than melee (23 vs 15 damage) on barely half the health (380 vs 650), while melee are worth nearly double the gold and XP (19–31g / 68 XP vs 12–24g / 35 XP). Which ones you kill, and in what order, is the actual mechanic of wave control:

  • To shove: kill the three ranged first. They carry most of the wave's damage output — remove them and your wave wins the push on its own; the tanky melee just die slower while yours do the work. AoE the back line, then clean up.
  • To freeze: the ranged minions ARE the battery. The ~3–4 extra enemy casters you preserve outdamage your wave from 600 range and keep it pressing toward you. Kill them and you killed your own freeze — last-hit melee freely, touch ranged only for the killing blow.
  • Trading runs through minion aggro. Damaging an enemy hero draws their minions onto you for ~2 seconds — three casters at 23 damage a hit is a real health tax early. Trade from outside their caster line, after their casters are dead, or eat the aggro deliberately to drag a winning wave back (section below).
  • Under dive threat, secure the melee. If you can only safely take half the wave, the melee are worth roughly double — three melee is most of the wave's income.
  • Under tower, melee buy you time. Melee take only 27% damage from towers; ranged take 68% and evaporate. Catching a crashing wave means your casters are already dead — the melee are what's left to farm, and what holds the line while you do.
  • Siege waves set the crash clock. A siege minion joins every 3rd wave (every 2nd from 15:00, every wave from 25:00). The siege wave is the one to crash before an objective window — it pushes hardest and punishes towers — and the one an enemy freeze struggles to hold against.

Setting it up

  • You need their wave to be the bigger one, pushing toward you — the classic buffer is ~3–4 extra enemy caster minions. If your waves are even, a freeze isn't available yet; let them shove or trim your own wave's damage until it is.
  • Catch the wave before the tower does. The freeze point is just outside tower range — once minions take tower shots, the tower breaks the freeze for you. If the wave is about to crash, stand in front of it and body-block ("tank") the extra minions for a few seconds to hold it where you want it.
  • Stand outside minion aggro while you wait — close enough to last-hit, far enough that their minions aren't chewing your wave down and un-balancing the freeze.

Holding it

  • Last-hit at the last possible moment, and touch nothing else. Every point of damage you put into their wave beyond the killing blow pushes the wave away from you.
  • Match their poke into your wave. If they auto or splash your minions to break the freeze, mirror that damage into theirs — both waves then die at the same rate and the line holds. An unanswered splash is how most freezes quietly die.
  • If your wave starts winning, take minion aggro for a moment (auto their laner or walk into the caster line) and drag it back — bleeding your own wave keeps theirs bigger and the freeze alive.

When to freeze — and when not to

  • Freeze when: you're ahead and want to zone them off the wave entirely (they step up, they die — that's the bully loop); you're behind in the offlane's losing state and want safe farm with gank insurance; or their jungler just showed on the far side and your laner has to choose between bleeding CS and walking into a camped lane.
  • Don't freeze when: an objective window is about to open — the clock (section 1) wants the wave crashed before Fangtooth/Prime so you can rotate without paying the lane; when your win condition is roaming (a freeze chains you to the lane — it's a commitment, not a parking brake); or into a waveclear mage who resets it for free.
  • Against an enemy freeze: don't bleed CS into it. Hard-shove the full wave into their tower to reset the lane, bring your jungler to crash it, or accept the loss and take the trade elsewhere — river, camps, a roam their frozen laner can't answer.

Crash before the objective window, freeze between them. The wave state and the clock are one decision, not two.

5 · Build like the top of the ladder builds

High-level players don't memorize builds — they hold a small grammar of reasons. Every serious guide in the catalog writes builds the same five ways:

  • The swap grammar: {default} → {swap} when {condition}. The canonical conditions: a cleanse item over the damage opener against heavy CC; anti-heal against sustain; percent-health damage against health-stackers; penetration against armor-stackers; the last one or two slots always flexed to the enemy comp.
  • Count, don't feel. The best guides make defensive choices numeric: tenacity builds "when the enemy has lots of stuns to interrupt your ultimate — against one or fewer, go damage"; the CC item "if the enemy has 1+ hard-CC abilities." Count the enemy kit in the draft, then itemize.
  • Name the engine. The most respected builds are organized around one scaling engine stated in a sentence — health stacking feeding a health-to-power item, a burn feeding healing procs, a haste rush feeding a gold engine. Every slot either feeds the engine or answers a threat; a slot that does neither is a wasted purchase.
  • Don't build away from your kit. An attack-speed build on an ability hero "loses all the skills she's so powerful with." The kit's cast rate picks your proc items; the kit's mechanism picks your stats.
  • Slots are liquid. The hold-and-upgrade idiom (buy the cheap stacking component second, complete it last), selling the early mana trinket late, selling the gold engine at full build, trading utility for survivability once cooldowns stop mattering — a build is a process across the whole game, not a checklist.
Two more axes the ladder underuses: fight length and stated greed

Fight length is a real itemization axis: burst mitigation when you expect to get blown up, lifesteal when you expect the long fight. Ask which fight your next game will actually contain.

Greed is conditional and should be said out loud. "This is the optimal build, but you need to be ahead — if you fall behind you need to change." A greedy build isn't wrong in general; it's wrong for a game state. Re-check the condition every recall.

6 · The splitpush doctrine

Splitpushing is the most misplayed macro tool on the ladder, and the community's one dedicated macro guide lays out the actual doctrine:

  • Prepare before the window. Proxfarm — stack the mid and side waves behind the tier-1 — before the Fangtooth window opens, so your pressure peaks exactly when the map fight starts.
  • The stay/join rule is numeric. Keep pushing while 2+ enemies commit to stopping you — that's a 4v3 for your team on the other side. The moment fewer than two watch you, your push is free real estate for them somewhere else.
  • "If you're late for splitpush → teamfight." Arriving to split after the fight window opened is the worst of both — you miss the fight and the towers.
  • Sustain off nearby camps, not recalls. A recall resets the whole threat.
  • It's a team play. The split only pays if the four are pressuring something simultaneously. A splitter with a passive team isn't a strategy, it's a 4v5.

And from the other side: when the enemy splits, the same math applies in reverse — send exactly one answer, and take the 4v4 fight or the objective their splitter isn't at.

7 · Micro that's assumed up here

Above Gold, this is table stakes — opponents are doing all of it:

  • Animation canceling — cutting an ability's follow-through the moment its damage lands. It compounds: faster clears, faster objectives, more damage per fight window. Learn it in the practice tool, per kit.
  • Auto-reset weaving — attack → ability → attack, resetting the swing timer. Free damage on every trade.
  • Passive upkeep — dragging camps to chain them, kiting a buff between camps to keep stacks rolling, spacing casts so proc effects never idle.
  • Cooldown accounting in trades — "burn their Blink" then kill on the timer; wait out the ability that answers your engage (the tether, the reflect, the cleanse) before committing yours.
The fine print that decides close fights (community-reported mechanics)
  • Slow stacking is not additive — the strongest slow applies plus a small bonus per extra source. Don't build stacked slows.
  • Anti-heal sources don't stack multiplicatively — but vector coverage matters: an on-hit anti-heal never touches a hero you can't auto, so a second (ability-applied) source can still be right.
  • Know your immunity gaps — some ultimates do not grant CC immunity while channeling. Know which of yours can be interrupted, and which of theirs you can interrupt.
  • Reflects and blocks have exceptions — know the specific abilities your defensive tool cannot answer before you bet a fight on it.
  • Stat caps are real build targets — attack-speed caps, 100% crit completion. A stat past its cap is gold burned; high-elo players build to the threshold, then stop.

8 · Review your own film — the interrogation

Every rank above Platinum reviews film. Don't rewatch to relive it — interrogate it. The questions a real coach asks of a lost game, in order:

  • Which fights did we win and not cash? What was takeable in each window?
  • For every objective they took: who was dead, and why? If nobody was dead — where were the five of us, and who saw it happen?
  • Which deaths directly preceded an enemy tower or objective? Those are the ones that cost; the rest were even trades.
  • Was the losing stretch a fight problem or a numbers problem? Count the bodies at each engage — a 4v5 lost is a call error by the four, not a mechanics error by the one.
  • Which fights were taken items-down, and what would waiting two minutes have changed?
  • What did the vision war look like around each major — wards placed, wards cleared, and by whom?

The Coach runs exactly this interrogation over your squad's real matches — the fights, the windows, the causes, and the build reasoning per player — so the review happens even when nobody feels like doing it.

Everything gets a cause or it doesn't go in the review. "We lost" is not analysis; "their 23-minute Fangtooth fell with all five of us alive" is.
